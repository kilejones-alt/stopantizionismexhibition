Five Timeline Positions

A Genealogy of Modern Antisemitism

Modern antisemitism did not simply continue medieval Christian hostility under another name.

During the nineteenth century, older suspicions of Jews were reorganized through race, nationalism, pseudoscience, political conspiracy, economics, and mass politics.

Emancipation had allowed Jews to enter professions, universities, journalism, commerce, culture, and government. Antisemites interpreted that integration not as assimilation but as infiltration. The Jew could convert and remain a Jew. He could become a citizen and still be described as foreign. He could participate in modern society and be accused of secretly controlling it.

Jewish difference had been transformed from a problem of belief into a supposedly permanent problem of blood, power, and belonging.

Late Nineteenth Century — From Religion to Race

The language of race gave anti-Jewish hostility a new claim to permanence.

Christianity had traditionally offered conversion as an escape from Jewish religious error. Racial antisemitism closed that exit. A baptized Jew could still be classified as Jewish. At the same time, nationalism raised another question: could Jews ever truly belong to the nations in which they lived? The Jew increasingly appeared as a figure who was simultaneously rootless and clannish, assimilated and alien, weak and extraordinarily powerful.

Contradiction was not a weakness of antisemitism. It became one of its defining features.

1904 — Exile

Exile

Creator: Samuel Hirszenberg
Date: 1904

Creator

Samuel Hirszenberg was a Polish-Jewish realist and symbolist painter whose major works repeatedly addressed Jewish vulnerability, wandering, and exclusion.

Object

A long procession of Jewish men, women, and children moves behind an isolated elderly figure, turning forced movement and displacement into a monumental collective image.

Archive

Samuel Hirszenberg, Exile, 1904; public-domain reproduction, Wikimedia Commons.

1905 — Mourning

The Black Banner

Creator: Samuel Hirszenberg
Date: 1905

Creator

Hirszenberg painted The Black Banner amid intensified pogrom violence; the work was exhibited in Paris in 1906.

Object

A dense Jewish funeral procession advances beneath a dark sky, the black banner and compressed crowd turning mourning into a collective public image.

Archive

Samuel Hirszenberg, The Black Banner, 1905; The Jewish Museum, New York; public-domain reproduction via Wikimedia Commons.

1905 — Jewish Self-Defense

The Birth of Jewish Resistance

Creator: Lazar Krestin
Date: 1905

Creator

Lazar Krestin, a Lithuanian-born Jewish painter trained in Vilnius and Vienna, made the work in reaction to the wave of anti-Jewish riots following the 1903 Kishinev pogrom.

Object

Young Jewish men and women move forward armed with clubs and a pistol while older bearded figures recede into the background.

Archive

Lazar Krestin, The Birth of Jewish Resistance, 1905; Magnes Collection of Jewish Art and Life; public-domain image via Wikimedia Commons.

c. 1910 — After the Pogrom

After the Pogrom

Creator: Maurycy Minkowski
Date: c. 1910

Creator

Maurycy Minkowski was a Polish-Jewish painter who traveled to areas affected by pogroms and made Jewish displacement and suffering central subjects of his work.

Object

Displaced women, children, and an injured man sit together after a pogrom, their stillness and physical proximity conveying shock rather than spectacle.

Archive

Maurycy Minkowski, After the Pogrom, c. 1910, oil on canvas; The Jewish Museum, New York; public-domain reproduction.

1917 — The Ruined Shtetl

Small Town after the Pogrom

Creator: Issachar Ber Ryback
Date: 1917

Creator

Issachar Ber Ryback was a Jewish modernist associated with the Kultur-Lige. His early work drew on the towns, synagogues, and visual culture of Eastern European Jewish life.

Object

Buildings collapse into jagged planes in Ryback’s vision of a small Jewish town after a pogrom, with the shtetl itself appearing physically shattered.

Archive

Issachar Ber Ryback, Small Town after the Pogrom, 1917; public-domain image via Wikimedia Commons.

Closing

Antijudaism had imagined the Jew as the enemy of Christian truth. Modern antisemitism recast him as the enemy of nation, race, social order, and civilization. Collective guilt, hidden agency, contamination, divided loyalty, ritualized accusation, and conspiracy provided fodder for a seemingly endless amount of hatred.

The Jew would eventually be joined by another figure:

the Zionist.
