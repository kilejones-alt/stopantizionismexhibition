Antisemitism

A Genealogy of Modern Antisemitism

Modern antisemitism did not simply continue medieval Christian hostility under another name.

During the nineteenth century, older suspicions of Jews were reorganized through race, nationalism, pseudoscience, political conspiracy, economics, and mass politics.

Emancipation had allowed Jews to enter professions, universities, journalism, commerce, culture, and government. Antisemites interpreted that integration not as assimilation but as infiltration. The Jew could convert and remain a Jew. He could become a citizen and still be described as foreign. He could participate in modern society and be accused of secretly controlling it.

Jewish difference had been transformed from a problem of belief into a supposedly permanent problem of blood, power, and belonging.

Late Nineteenth Century — From Religion to Race

The language of race gave anti-Jewish hostility a new claim to permanence.

Christianity had traditionally offered conversion as an escape from Jewish religious error. Racial antisemitism closed that exit. A baptized Jew could still be classified as Jewish. At the same time, nationalism raised another question: could Jews ever truly belong to the nations in which they lived? The Jew increasingly appeared as a figure who was simultaneously rootless and clannish, assimilated and alien, weak and extraordinarily powerful.

Contradiction was not a weakness of antisemitism. It became one of its defining features.

Exile

—1904
1904 — Exile

Exile

Creator: Samuel Hirszenberg
Date: 1904

Samuel Hirszenberg, a Polish-Jewish painter working between realism and symbolism, returned throughout his major oeuvre to Jewish precarity, exile, and exclusion.

Samuel Hirszenberg's Exile emerged from a world in which those abstractions had acquired physical consequences. Jewish communities in Eastern Europe lived amid legal discrimination, agitation, pogrom violence, and recurring displacement.

The long procession in Hirszenberg's painting transforms exile from a theological memory into a modern political condition. The modern Jew could possess a home, a town, a profession, and citizenship and still discover that belonging could be revoked.

Antisemitism turned the Jew into the permanent foreigner.

The Black Banner

—1905
1905 — Mourning

The Black Banner

Creator: Samuel Hirszenberg
Date: 1905

Hirszenberg conceived The Black Banner amid escalating pogrom violence; the painting was subsequently exhibited in Paris in 1906.

Hirszenberg's The Black Banner converts the aftermath of pogrom violence into a collective funeral.

The pogrom was not simply an eruption of private hatred. Modern anti-Jewish violence could be encouraged by newspapers, political movements, rumors, officials, and various campaigns. The Jew was blamed for capitalism and revolution, wealth and poverty, liberalism and radicalism, national defeat and social disorder.

A population could be attacked for something an individual Jew had done, and what “the Jews” were said to represent.

The Birth of Jewish Resistance

—1905
1905 — Jewish Self-Defense

The Birth of Jewish Resistance

Creator: Lazar Krestin
Date: 1905

Lazar Krestin, a Lithuanian-born Jewish painter trained in Vilnius and Vienna, created the work in response to the wave of anti-Jewish violence that followed the 1903 Kishinev pogrom.

Lazar Krestin's The Birth of Jewish Resistance records the response to that transformation.

After Kishinev and subsequent pogroms, Jewish self-defense organizations appeared in communities across the Russian Empire. Young Jews armed themselves because the state could not always be relied upon to protect them. The image marks an important change in Jewish political consciousness. Persecution was no longer understood only as a catastrophe to be endured. It became something to resist.

Antisemitism helped generate several competing Jewish answers to the same problem: emigration, socialism, Bundism, religious renewal, self-defense, and Zionism.

After the Pogrom

—c. 1910
c. 1910 — After the Pogrom

After the Pogrom

Creator: Maurycy Minkowski
Date: c. 1910

Maurycy Minkowski, a Polish-Jewish painter, traveled through communities scarred by pogroms and made displacement and Jewish suffering central subjects of his art.

Maurycy Minkowski's After the Pogrom turns away from the violence itself.

Its subject is what remains afterward: displaced families, injury, exhaustion, and the destruction of ordinary security. That aftermath matters because antisemitism operated not only through murder. It worked through intimidation, exclusion, dispossession, professional restriction, forced migration, humiliation, and the knowledge that violence might return.

The pogrom made a proposition physical: Jews could live within society while never being entirely secure inside it.

Small Town after the Pogrom

—1917
1917 — The Ruined Shtetl

Small Town after the Pogrom

Creator: Issachar Ber Ryback
Date: 1917

Issachar Ber Ryback, a Jewish modernist associated with the Kultur-Lige, drew in his early work on the towns, synagogues, and vernacular visual culture of Eastern European Jewish life.

Issachar Ber Ryback's Small Town after the Pogrom makes the Jewish town appear broken.

By the First World War and the revolutions that followed, Jewish communities across Eastern Europe were caught between armies, collapsing empires, nationalist movements, revolutionary politics, and waves of anti-Jewish violence.

The destruction of the shtetl was simultaneously physical and symbolic. The modern antisemite no longer needed medieval theology to explain why Jews were dangerous.

The Jew could now be accused of being a racially impure, revolutionary conspirator, capitalist exploiter, cosmopolitan subversive, national traitor, or hidden ruler.
