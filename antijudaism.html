Antijudaism

A Genealogy of Christian Antijudaism

Antijudaism preceded modern antisemitism by centuries.

Its central language was theological. Jews were represented as spiritually blind, collectively guilty, stubborn in their rejection of Christianity, and dangerous to Christian society. Over time, doctrine became image, accusation, law, folklore, and violence.

The underlying structure endured: Jews were imagined not simply as people who practiced another religion, but as an alien presence capable of corrupting, contaminating, or attacking the sacred order around them.

Synagoga, Strasbourg Cathedral

—c. 1230
c. 1230 — Supersession

Synagoga, Strasbourg Cathedral

Creator: Unknown Gothic sculptor; photograph by Rama
Date: c. 1230 (sculpture); 2006 photograph

The sculptural ensemble at Strasbourg Cathedral mobilizes personification to give monumental form to the supersessionist claim that the Church had displaced Judaism.

At Strasbourg Cathedral, the paired figures of Ecclesia and Synagoga rendered a theological argument in human form.

Ecclesia, representing the Church, appears triumphant. Synagoga, representing Judaism, is defeated and blindfolded. The image expressed the Christian doctrine that the Church had superseded Israel. Judaism survived, but within this visual theology it survived as a faith that could no longer see the truth before it.

Süßkind of Trimberg, Codex Manesse

—between 1305 and 1340
1305–1340 — Marked Difference

Süßkind of Trimberg, Codex Manesse

Creator: Master of the Codex Manesse, Additional Painter I
Date: between 1305 and 1340

The Codex Manesse is a lavishly illuminated anthology of Middle High German lyric verse compiled in Heidelberg; Süßkind is the manuscript’s sole identified Jewish poet.

The Codex Manesse portrait of the Jewish poet Süßkind of Trimberg belongs to a different visual world, but it reveals another development: the Jew made visibly distinct.

Süßkind appears within the courtly culture of medieval Germany while being marked through features associated with Jewish identity, most notably the pointed Jewish hat.

Across medieval Europe, clothing regulations and visual conventions increasingly made Jewish differences visible. The Jew was not merely someone who believed differently. He could be recognized, marked, and separated.

Burning of Jews during the Black Death

—1349
1349 — Pestilence and Collective Guilt

Burning of Jews during the Black Death

Creator: Medieval manuscript illuminator
Date: 1349

This image survives within the visual archive of persecutions that accompanied the Black Death, when plague panic converged with anti-Jewish accusation and violence across parts of Europe.

The Black Death produced one of medieval Europe's most catastrophic waves of anti-Jewish violence.

As plague devastated communities, Jews were accused of poisoning wells and deliberately spreading disease. Jewish communities were massacred even in places where authorities attempted to deny or suppress the accusation. A natural catastrophe was caused by a human conspirator.

The accusation established a durable pattern: when society suffered from forces that were frightening, invisible, and difficult to explain, Jews could be imagined as the hidden authors of the disaster.

Miracle of the Profaned Host

—1467–1468
1467–1468

Miracle of the Profaned Host

Creator: Paolo Uccello
Date: 1467–1468

Paolo Uccello, among the most distinctive painters of early Renaissance Florence, executed this six-panel predella for the Corpus Domini confraternity in Urbino.

The host-desecration libel transformed Christian theology into recurring Jewish violence.

Stories claimed that Jews obtained consecrated Eucharistic hosts in order to stab, burn, mutilate, or otherwise attack them. Because the host represented the body of Christ, the alleged act recreated the Crucifixion. Paolo Uccello's Miracle of the Profaned Host placed this accusation within one of Renaissance Italy's most sophisticated visual cultures.

The Jew was imagined as unable to stop attacking Christ even centuries after his death.

Theological rejection had become ritual aggression.

Simon of Trent in the Nuremberg Chronicle

—1493
1493 — Ritual Murder

Simon of Trent in the Nuremberg Chronicle

Creator: Workshop of Michael Wolgemut and Wilhelm Pleydenwurff
Date: 1493

A landmark of early European print culture, the Nuremberg Chronicle was compiled by Hartmann Schedel and illustrated in the Wolgemut-Pleydenwurff workshop.

The accusation surrounding Simon of Trent belonged to the blood-libel tradition: the false claim that Jews murdered Christian children for religious purposes.

After Simon disappeared in 1475, members of Trent's Jewish community were arrested, tortured, convicted, and executed. The story circulated through sermons, images, chronicles, and culture. The Nuremberg Chronicle helped give this a durable visual form.

Here several older themes converged: Jewish secrecy, collective guilt, hatred of Christianity, ritual violence, and danger to children. The Jew was no longer merely an enemy of Christian truth.

He could be imagined as the enemy of Christianity.

LIBELS
01

Well-Poisoning

As plague devastated communities, Jews were accused of poisoning wells and deliberately spreading disease. Jewish communities were massacred even in places where authorities attempted to deny or suppress the accusation. A natural catastrophe was caused by a human conspirator.

02

Deicide Libel

The deicide libel held Jews collectively and hereditarily responsible for the death of Jesus. A Roman execution in first-century Judea was recast as an enduring Jewish crime, turning a theological accusation into collective guilt applied to Jews across generations.

03

Blood Libel

The accusation surrounding Simon of Trent belonged to the blood-libel tradition: the false claim that Jews murdered Christian children for religious purposes. After Simon disappeared in 1475, members of Trent's Jewish community were arrested, tortured, convicted, and executed.
