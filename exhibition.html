A Genealogy of Modern Antizionism

Modern antizionism did not suddenly appear after 1967.

Its principal ideas were assembled across decades through revolutionary Marxism, Soviet state doctrine, Arab nationalism, Palestinian political thought, and international institutions. Terms, images, and political habits passed from one setting into another until Zionism could be presented as colonialism, racism, conspiracy, fascism, and eventually genocide.

THREE INFLUENCES
01

The Arab world

where opposition to Jewish national self-determination became a central political and ideological movement.

02

Nazi ideology

whose propaganda and demonization of Jews continued to influence anti-Jewish narratives after the Second World War.

03

The Soviet Union

which transformed traditional antisemitic narratives into the language of anti-Zionism, anti-colonialism, and political activism, exporting these ideas throughout the world.

The Degradation of Alfred Dreyfus

—1895
Dreyfus Affair

The Degradation of Alfred Dreyfus

Creator: Henri Meyer / Le Petit Journal
Date: 1895

Henri Meyer, a French illustrator and caricaturist, rendered the degradation of Dreyfus for the illustrated supplement of Le Petit Journal.

Meyer turns a military ritual into public spectacle: uniformed authority fills the frame while Dreyfus is held nearly motionless at its center. The crowd is integral to the image because degradation acquires political force when it is made visible, witnessed, and socially ratified. The illustration records the choreography by which an institution teaches spectators what disgrace is supposed to look like.

Captain Alfred Dreyfus stands at the École Militaire in Paris while an officer breaks his sword and strips him of rank.

The broken sword condenses accusation into a single visual act. More than punishment is staged here: the ceremony dramatizes the boundary of national belonging by showing, before an audience, who can be cast outside it. Rank, honor, and civic standing are made to appear revocable in the same instant.

1903 — Lenin and Jewish Nationhood

In “The Position of the Bund in the Party,” Vladimir Lenin rejected the conception of Jews as a separate nation and associated Jewish nationalism with Zionism. The dispute concerned more than party organization. It raised a question that would remain central to communist thought: whether Jews constituted a national people entitled to a political destiny of their own.

Marxist hostility toward Jewish nationalism supplied an early premise for later Soviet antizionism.

Kishinev Massacre Elegy

—1904
Kishinev Pogrom

Kishinev Massacre Elegy

Creator: Herman S. Shapiro
Date: 1904

Herman S. Shapiro composed and arranged this elegy in New York in 1904, one year after the Kishinev pogrom.

Published in New York one year after the pogrom, the sheet music shows catastrophe entering diasporic cultural circulation, where mourning became memory, testimony, and performance. Music carried the event beyond Bessarabia before later institutions of mass commemoration existed. The object therefore belongs simultaneously to the history of composition and to the history of how dispersed communities learned to preserve a recent trauma.

A red sheet-music cover surrounds scenes of violence from the Kishinev pogrom and a portrait of composer Herman S. Shapiro.

Portrait, ornament, and scenes of violence are compressed into a single memorial surface. The design denies aesthetic distance: private lament and collective catastrophe occupy the same field, making the act of looking inseparable from remembrance. The cover functions less as decoration than as a portable memorial, asking the viewer to encounter violence before the music has even begun.

Marxism and the National and Colonial Question

—1935
1913

Lenin’s Critical Remarks on the National Question

Creator: Joseph Stalin; 1935 Moscow edition
Date: 1935 edition; includes the 1913 national-question essay

The volume assembles Stalin’s writings and speeches on nationality and colonial questions, including his 1913 intervention on the national question.

The later Moscow edition is itself revealing: Bolshevik nationality theory was preserved as doctrine, giving earlier arguments about nationhood a durable institutional afterlife. Placed beside Lenin’s interventions, the volume shows how disputes first conducted as revolutionary theory could become a stable grammar for classifying peoples, nations, and national movements.

Lenin returned to Jewish nationhood in Critical Remarks on the National Question, defending assimilation and opposing Jewish nationalism.

Joseph Stalin's Marxism and the National Question, written in the same period, placed Zionism within the Bolshevik discussion of which peoples counted as nations and which national movements deserved political legitimacy.

Read together, the texts expose a problem of political classification: which collectivities counted as nations and which national movements were granted legitimacy. The dispute over Jewish nationhood therefore sits inside a larger Bolshevik effort to translate identity into administrable ideological categories.

Theses Presented to the Second World Congress

—1920
1920

Theses on the National and Colonial Questions

Creator: Communist International (Comintern)
Date: 1920

Issued for the Second World Congress of the Communist International, the volume includes its theses on national and colonial questions.

The congress volume fixes revolutionary argument in programmatic form: nation, colony, and liberation become categories by which political movements are admitted to—or excluded from—legitimacy. In documents of this kind, theory becomes administrative vision: the vocabulary used to classify liberation movements also determines which forms of nationalism appear progressive, reactionary, or suspect.

At the Second Congress of the Communist International, the “Theses on the National and Colonial Question” situated Zionism within the politics of British imperial rule in Palestine. A durable association entered revolutionary politics: Zionism and colonialism.

Zionism could now be read not primarily as a Jewish response to persecution or a movement for national self-government, but as an adjunct of imperial power.

The Old School / The Soviet School

—1920–1932
1920–32 — Sovietization of Jewish Life

The Old School / The Soviet School

Creator: Soviet state printing; specific agency unknown
Date: 1920–1932

An anonymous Soviet artist designed this Yiddish poster during campaigns to transfer Jewish children from religious schools into state education.

The poster belongs to a Soviet visual language in which modernization meant more than social improvement: it implied a transfer of allegiance from inherited communal institutions to the state. The child at the center becomes the hinge between two orders of authority, one communal and inherited, the other revolutionary and state-directed.

A Yiddish poster contrasts a traditional cheder and Jewish occupations with Soviet schools, factories, farms, and vocational training.

The contrast is categorical rather than neutral: the old Jewish world is pictured as residue, while school, factory, and farm are arranged as the architecture of the Soviet future. The poster’s pedagogy is visual before it is verbal; decline is fragmented and dim, progress ordered and bright, so political choice is presented with the force of common sense.

Zionism as a Crocodile

—1936
1936 — Zionism and Imperial Power

Zionism as a Crocodile

Creator: Falastin newspaper
Date: 1936

The Arabic-language newspaper Falastin published this crocodile cartoon in Jaffa on 18 June 1936.

The cartoon uses caricature as political compression: Zionism becomes a predatory body, translating disputes over land, migration, sovereignty, and power into an immediately legible drama of menace. The device is effective precisely because metaphor substitutes moral certainty for political complexity.

A crocodile marked as Zionism faces Palestinian Arabs while a British officer stands above it. The crocodile says it will swallow them “peacefully.”

The metaphor joins appetite to imperial sponsorship. By placing the British figure above the scene, the image presents the imagined threat not as isolated but as sheltered by a larger structure of power; domination is made to look coordinated before it is argued in words.

“Nazis Affiliate with Arabs; To Boycott Palestine Goods”

—1937
1937 — Nazi Outreach to Arab Nationalist Networks

“Nazis Affiliate with Arabs; To Boycott Palestine Goods”

Creator: The Sentinel / Jewish Telegraphic Agency dispatch
Date: 1937

The Chicago Jewish weekly The Sentinel printed this Jewish Telegraphic Agency dispatch from Damascus in its 9 December 1937 edition.

Its newspaper form matters: the episode reached readers not as retrospective history but as contemporary intelligence, framed through the terse authority of wire-service reporting. That immediacy matters because the clipping preserves the episode before later narratives could stabilize it, with contingency and uncertainty still visible in the language of reportage.

The report described a visit to the Near East by Baldur von Schirach, head of the Hitler Youth, accompanied by fifteen Nazi agents. It stated that German representatives discussed closer contact with Arab youth organizations and expanded German propaganda activity through schools in the region.

The same dispatch reported that an Arab National Youth Organization threatened a boycott of merchants who bought “Zionist goods from Palestine.” The clipping documents a prewar point of contact between Nazi political outreach and a specific Arab nationalist anti-Zionist campaign; it does not represent Arab society as a whole.

Amin al-Husseini Meets Adolf Hitler

—1941
1941

Amin al-Husseini Meets Adolf Hitler

Creator: Heinrich Hoffmann / German Federal Archive
Date: 1941

Heinrich Hoffmann, Hitler’s official photographer, recorded the meeting on 28 November 1941.

Hoffmann’s camera places the meeting within the visual machinery of the Nazi state, turning diplomacy into an image of proximity, recognition, and political access. The photograph’s formality also matters: the encounter is not pictured as clandestine but as a recognized meeting conducted within the representational conventions of state diplomacy.

During the Second World War, Hajj Amin al-Husayni, the Grand Mufti of Jerusalem, allied himself with Nazi Germany after leaving British-controlled Palestine and Iraq.

From Europe he participated in Arabic-language broadcasts directed toward the Middle East, attacking Britain, Jews, and Zionism while promoting the Axis cause.

Barid al-Sharq

—1941–44
1941–44 — Nazi Propaganda in Arabic

Barid al-Sharq

Creator: Reich Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda
Date: Illustrated run documented through 1941–44

Barid al-Sharq was an illustrated Arabic-language periodical produced in Berlin within the Reich propaganda apparatus and directed toward readers across the Arab world.

Produced in Berlin, the periodical occupied the intersection of wartime propaganda and Arabic-language political communication, adapting Axis messaging to a regional readership. Its existence shows how propaganda crosses linguistic boundaries by borrowing local idiom and familiar political symbols, seeking not merely to inform an audience but to recruit its interpretive habits.

An Arabic-language cover places a photograph of Amin al-Husseini with Hitler beneath the masthead Barid al-Sharq.

The cover makes association visible before any article is read: al-Husayni and Hitler are joined beneath a single Arabic masthead, converting alliance into graphic fact. The design performs argumentative work through proximity alone: faces, masthead, and political authority are made to reinforce one another without requiring lengthy textual explanation.

Al-Husayni with German SS and Bosnian Waffen-SS Personnel

—1943
1943

Nazi–Arab Collaboration

Creator: Photographer unidentified / United States Holocaust Memorial Museum collection
Date: 1943

USHMM does not identify the photographer in its catalog record. Preserved in the Museum’s collection courtesy of Robert Kempner, the photograph records Hajj Amin al-Husayni during a wartime encounter with German SS personnel and Bosnian members of the Waffen-SS.

By 1943, al-Husayni’s collaboration with Nazi Germany extended beyond diplomacy and Arabic-language broadcasting to support for the recruitment of Bosnian Muslims into Waffen-SS formations.

The photograph records an intersection between Nazi wartime strategy and al-Husayni’s anti-British, anti-Jewish, and anti-Zionist politics. The uniformed Bosnian Muslims pictured here were not Arab troops; the Arab political figure in the scene is al-Husayni.

“Soldats Arabes!”

—1941–1945
1941–45 — German Propaganda to Arab Soldiers

“Soldats Arabes!”

Creator: German psychological-warfare operations
Date: 1941–1945

German psychological-warfare units prepared this leaflet for Arabic-speaking soldiers in North Africa.

Its brevity is functional: propaganda intended for soldiers compresses ideology into a portable tactical message, designed to be understood before it can be examined at length.

Arabic and French text tells Arab soldiers that Germany is at war with Jews, calls Germany a friend of Arab peoples, and invokes the Grand Mufti of Jerusalem.

The leaflet asks readers to collapse separate theaters of war into a common political identity, aligning local grievance with the ideological categories of the German war effort.

1948–1953 — The Anti-Cosmopolitan Campaign

Late Stalinism introduced one of the most revealing political euphemisms of the period: the “rootless cosmopolitan.”

The term was directed disproportionately against Jewish writers, intellectuals, critics, and public figures. Jews were depicted as culturally alien, insufficiently patriotic, beholden to foreign interests, and detached from the national community.

The word Jew did not always need to be spoken.

This campaign marks a direct passage between older images of Jewish disloyalty and the later Soviet use of Zionist as a designation for foreign allegiance, conspiracy, and treason.

Peretz Markish after Arrest

—1949
1949–1950

Toward the Night of the Murdered Poets

Creator: USSR Ministry for State Security (MGB)
Date: 1949

The Soviet Ministry for State Security produced this identification photograph after arresting Peretz Markish, the Yiddish poet and member of the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee’s presidium.

The arrest photograph belongs to the bureaucratic archive of repression: a cultural figure is translated into an administrative image, stripped of the public identity his writing had sustained. That transformation—from writer to file, from public voice to numbered subject—is one of the defining visual signatures of modern political repression.

The Soviet government dissolved the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee in 1948.

Created during the war to rally Jewish support for the Soviet struggle against Nazi Germany, the committee had cultivated connections with Jewish communities abroad. Under Stalin, those connections became grounds for political suspicion.

Its leading members were arrested, interrogated, and prosecuted as Soviet authorities assaulted independent Jewish cultural life.

The Slánský Trial

—1952
1952

Slánský Trial: Zionism Criminalized

Date: 1952

The supplied reproduction carries no verified photographer credit. It documents the Prague show trial of 20–27 November 1952, in which Rudolf Slánský and thirteen other senior Communist officials were prosecuted; eleven of the fourteen defendants were Jewish.

The Slánský Trial in communist Czechoslovakia gave Zionist an especially sinister political meaning.

Fourteen senior Communist officials were prosecuted; eleven were Jewish. The case joined Zionism to espionage, treason, Western imperialism, financial intrigue, and secret foreign allegiance.

The word no longer required actual participation in Zionist politics.

1952 — The Night of the Murdered Poets

On August 12, 1952 , thirteen Soviet Jewish defendants associated with the Jewish Anti-Fascist Committee were executed. Among them were leading writers and intellectuals of Soviet Yiddish culture.

Their deaths marked the destruction of an extraordinary center of Jewish cultural life and belonged to Stalin's wider assault upon Jewish autonomy, international connections, and collective consciousness.

Decree Awarding Lidiya Timashuk the Order of Lenin

—1953
1953 — The Doctors' Plot

Decree Awarding Lidiya Timashuk the Order of Lenin

Creator: Presidium of the Supreme Soviet of the USSR
Date: 1953

The Presidium of the Supreme Soviet promulgated this decree on 20 January 1953, at the height of the Doctors’ Plot campaign.

The decree’s official form gives denunciation the appearance of civic virtue: the machinery of state honor operates alongside the machinery of accusation. The document shows how paper authority can normalize a campaign: reward, accusation, and bureaucratic procedure are joined within the same official visual register.

In January 1953, Soviet authorities announced that a group of prominent physicians, most of them Jewish, had conspired to murder Soviet leaders. The Doctors' Plot revived one of the oldest images in anti-Jewish history: Jews penetrating the intimate institutions of society in order to kill from within.

The setting was modern medicine rather than medieval ritual. Stalin died in March. The case was abandoned soon afterward.

Fayez Sayegh, Zionist Colonialism in Palestine

—1965
1965

Sayegh and the Settler-Colonial Frame

Date: 1965

In 1965, Palestinian intellectual and diplomat Fayez Sayegh published Zionist Colonialism in Palestine through the PLO Research Center, a pivotal articulation of Zionism within an explicitly colonial frame.

The pamphlet’s significance lies partly in the portability of its vocabulary: a compact political text offered a conceptual frame able to travel readily through activist and diplomatic discourse. Its compact form also helped detach the frame from one immediate controversy and make it reusable as a general explanatory model for Zionism itself.

In 1965, Palestinian intellectual and diplomat Fayez Sayegh published Zionist Colonialism in Palestine through the PLO Research Center.

Sayegh placed Zionism squarely within the history of European colonial conquest and racial domination. The timing mattered. Colonial empires were collapsing, liberation movements had become central to postwar politics, and racism had become one of the defining evils of the time.

1967 — The Soviet Antizionist Campaign

Israel's victory in the Six-Day War inaugurated a far more extensive Soviet campaign. Antizionism ceased to operate as hostility toward Israeli foreign policy. Soviet publications developed an elaborate literature in which Zionism was linked with imperialism, racism, fascism, financial domination, conspiracy, militarism, and war.

This literature became known broadly as Zionology.

Older images of Jewish wealth, hidden influence, divided loyalty, and conspiracy could be reproduced under anti-racism and anti-imperialism.

In Poland, the state's “anti-Zionist” campaign removed Jews and people of Jewish ancestry from universities, the military, the media, government, and public life. Thousands were pressured into emigration.

Demonstration against Resolution 3379

—1975
1975 — “Zionism Is Racism”

Demonstration against Resolution 3379

Creator: IPPA photographer / Dan Hadani collection
Date: 1975

An IPPA photographer captured this 1975 demonstration; the image is preserved in the Dan Hadani press-photo collection at the National Library of Israel.

The photograph records public opposition at the moment a contested political definition entered institutional language, binding the history of the resolution to the history of those who resisted what that language would make politically thinkable.

On November 10, 1975, the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution 3379, declaring Zionism “a form of racism and racial discrimination.”

The vote represented an international triumph for decades of Soviet, Arab nationalist, Palestinian, and Third World efforts to identify Zionism with the great moral lapses of the postwar world.

Jewish collective expression had now been placed inside the category of racism by the largest political body in the world.

Durban Conference

—2001
2001 — Durban

Durban Conference

Date: 2001

No photographer is identified in the supplied reproduction. The image documents demonstrations surrounding the United Nations World Conference Against Racism in Durban, South Africa, held from 31 August to 8 September 2001, amid extensive NGO and activist mobilization focused on Israel and Zionism.

The World Conference against Racism in Durban became another major site in the history of antizionism.

The conference and its surrounding NGO activity placed Israel and Zionism at the center of condemnations over racism, apartheid, colonialism, and human rights.

Language once carried principally through Soviet propaganda, Arab state politics, and international diplomacy now moved through NGOs, universities, activist networks, and human-rights organizations.

The same themes—racism, colonialism, conspiracy, and Jewish power—reappeared in new institutions.

LIBELS
01

Settler-Colonialism

Settler-colonialism removes Zionism from the history of Jewish nationhood and places it within the history of European conquest.

Jewish return becomes foreign settlement. Jewish continuity in the land becomes secondary to an account in which Zionism begins as an imported European project imposed upon an indigenous population.

A pivotal text appeared in 1965, when Palestinian intellectual and diplomat Fayez Sayegh published Zionist Colonialism in Palestine through the PLO Research Center.

Sayegh wrote during the great era of decolonization, when European empires were retreating across Africa and Asia and colonial rule had become synonymous with dispossession, racial hierarchy, and foreign domination.

02

Apartheid

Apartheid converts political conflict into racial ontology. The word carries the memory of South Africa's system of legally enforced white supremacy. Applied to Israel in its maximal sense, it does not merely condemn discrimination, military occupation, unequal laws, or particular policies. It identifies Jewish sovereignty itself with racial domination. A state regarded as apartheid in its essence cannot be repaired by ordinary politics. Its governing principle must be abolished.

03

Genocide

The term names the intentional destruction, in whole or in part, of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group. Its application to Zionism itself carries a claim far beyond civilian death, unlawful killing, military excess, or war crimes.

It attributes exterminatory purpose to Jewish sovereignty.

The inversion is historically profound. Zionism arose from the repeated failure of European emancipation and became urgent amid the destruction of European Jewry. In antizionist thought, the political answer to Jewish vulnerability can instead be cast as the instrument of annihilation.

The people upon whom genocide was inflicted become its exemplary perpetrators. Jewish refuge becomes colonial invasion. Jewish sovereignty becomes racial supremacy. Jewish self-defense becomes extermination.

Settler-colonialism, apartheid, and genocide are deployed together as an indictment of Zionism: illegitimate in origin, racist in essence, and evil in purpose. Theology had condemned Jews as the enemy of God. The racial Jew had been judged as the enemy of civilization. The Zionist is now viewed as the enemy of humanity.
